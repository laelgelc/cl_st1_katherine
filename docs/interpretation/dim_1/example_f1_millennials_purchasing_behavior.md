# Millennials’ Purchasing Behavior Toward Fashion Clothing Brands: Influence of Brand Awareness and Brand Schematicity

## Introduction

The worldwide fashion apparel market, worth US$3 trillion, makes up 2% of global gross domestic product (GDP) and employs 33 million textile workers, an increase from 19.7 million over the previous 25 years (Wang et al., 2019). Similarly, a report from *Clothing stores* (2019) acknowledged that global revenue in fashion retailing reached $1.7 trillion in 2017 and was expected to grow by 2% by 2022.

Despite the growth and importance of fashion retailing, only a limited number of studies focus on customers’ attitudes related to fashion clothing brands (Choi and Choo, 2016; Phau et al., 2015; Vehmas et al., 2018). Therefore, it is essential to understand brand-related consumers’ purchasing behavior more deeply to accelerate industry performance.

The current research considers millennials: young consumers born between 1980 and 2000 (Hartman and McCambridge, 2011). Empirical investigation shows that millennials represent the largest generational population globally, and their purchasing power makes them an attractive target for the fashion clothing brand industry (Moore and Carpenter, 2008; Gerardo, 2018). This segment also represents a significant portion of global fashion retail consumption (deKerviler and Rodriguez, 2019). Research shows that millennials are young, affluent, educated, tech-savvy, and inclined toward positive social behavior (Howe and Strauss, 2009).

However, academic literature has so far failed to acknowledge the connection among significant attributes that affect how millennials purchase fashion clothing brands (Samala and Singh, 2019). Although several studies explore diverse consumers and their behavior regarding fashion clothing brands, none have examined brand consciousness (BC), brand awareness (BA), brand nationality (BN), and brand schematicity (BS) in one framework to gain deeper insight into millennials’ purchasing behavior toward fashion clothing brands (MCPBFCB).

Authors refer to BC as consumers’ mental orientation toward purchasing well-known brand-name products (Rahman et al., 2020; Mann and Sahni, 2019). BS is a result of the consumer socialization process, which provides people with the “skills, knowledge and attitudes” relevant to consumption (Ward, 1974; Puligadda et al., 2012). Unique experiences with a particular brand influence socialization and may instill consumers with brand-based associations that serve as cognitive schemas and emphasize the value of brands in general (Rossiter, 1976).

According to Keller (2003), BA is consumers’ ability to identify a brand under different conditions in the form of brand recognition and brand recall. Researchers claim that to attract new customers and retain existing ones, firms need to distinguish themselves from competitors (Radder and Huang, 2008). Previous studies also suggest that country image activates concepts and knowledge that shape consumers’ interpretation and evaluation of products, thereby influencing assessments of an existing brand (Ahn et al., 2009; Papadopoulos and Heslop, 2002). Literature also provides evidence that consumers’ knowledge of BN or origin may significantly influence judgments of quality, brand attitudes, and purchasing behavior in the marketplace (Maheswaran, 1994).

Contemporary research suggests that the fashion industry is comprehensive in nature and encompasses the continuous expansion of consumer understanding regarding fashion trends, lifestyle, and cultural dimensions (Peng, 2020). As many fashion brands exist in the marketplace, purchase decisions follow a thorough evaluation, and consumers develop emotional relationships with brands (Liu and Chang, 2017). Therefore, consumers may assess a brand based on the cues of BA, BS, BC, and BN. However, empirical evidence on this topic remains limited.

In this research context, millennials with high BC toward fashion clothing brands believe that these brands reflect their personality (Ordun, 2015; Kaur and Anand, 2018). Moreover, millennials’ brand preferences have both direct and indirect effects on the purchasing behavior of their families and peers (Tang and Chan, 2017). Thus, the primary aim of this research is to empirically investigate MCPBFCB in relation to BA, BS, BC, and BN.

This paper begins with a literature review and formulates research hypotheses along with the development of a conceptual framework. It then presents the methods, findings, and theoretical and managerial implications. Finally, it discusses research limitations and future research directions.

## Literature Review and Hypotheses Development

Fashion is an activity through which individuals express personal tastes and interests through clothing. The conceptualization of fashion is considered an objective reflection of behavioral processes through which consumers present their lifestyles (Vieira, 2009). Fashion clothing is identified as a highly animated product category that communicates brand preference (O’Cass, 2004) and reflects the consumer’s self-image (DaMcNeill and Moore, 2015). Research on fashion has received particular attention in fashion marketing and brand management (O’Cass and Siahtiri, 2014; Kautish and Sharma, 2018; Sung and Woo, 2019). Fashion is an innovative industry characterized by dynamic changes resulting from technological advancements (Nash, 2019).

Prior research has extensively studied various dimensions to explain consumer behavior toward fashion clothing brands. To identify existing research, databases including Scopus, Web of Knowledge, Business Source Complete, Emerald, and ScienceDirect were searched using the keyword “fashion clothing brand.” The literature reveals that marketing scholars have mostly emphasized consumers’ fashion buying behaviors across various segments, including millennials and Generation Y. However, no prior study on fashion clothing brands has highlighted the combined role of BA and BS in understanding consumer purchasing behavior.

BS is a relatively new way to understand individual differences in processing brand-associated information, which substantially affects branding and marketing efforts (Puligadda et al., 2012). This study considers BS as an individual-difference phenomenon. The primary aim is to critically analyze the role of BS in understanding millennials’ purchasing behavior. Despite the importance of understanding consumers’ brand information processing at the individual level, there is limited empirical evidence on whether BS predicts MCPBFCB. Therefore, this study examines the role of BS in MCPBFCB while considering BA, BC, and BN in a single framework.

This study also acknowledges cognitive self-schema theory, which explains individual psychology based on how people perceive information developed over time and how that accumulated information drives decision-making (Halkias, 2015). Positive self-schema can moderate the conversion of intentions into actual behavior (Tikka and Oinas-Kukkonen, 2019), helping researchers understand the gap between intention and behavior (David et al., 2009). According to Samala and Singh (2019), schema knowledge helps create behavior by highlighting default choices or attractive attributes. In this study, the researchers identify attributes millennials consider when buying fashion items.

## Brand Awareness and Purchasing Behavior

BA is a fundamental driving force in consumer decision-making. Consumer decision-making for products or services is highly driven by BA, which refers to whether consumers can recognize and recall a brand under various circumstances (Aaker, 1996). Although BA and brand recognition are sometimes used interchangeably, brand recognition and brand recall are two pivotal dimensions of BA (Keller, 1993, 2009).

Brand recognition is based on aided recall, where consumers recognize brands from a given list based on prior exposure. Brand recall relies on unaided recall, where consumers retrieve brand information from memory based on industry prompts (Keller, 1993; Holden, 1993; Radder and Huang, 2008; Edelman, 2010). The first brand name that comes to a consumer’s mind when asked is an example of BA at the highest level (Laurent et al., 1995). BA is already formed in consumers’ minds (Foroudi et al., 2018).

BA acts as a primary driver that initiates brand resonance (Jung Jung et al., 2014). Demand for a brand is created by consumers’ BA and decision heuristics, which significantly influence decision-making (Cheung et al., 2019). Fashion clothing brands are distinctively experiential (Aaker, 1997), and consumer experience is shaped by confidence and connection, which increase BA (Huang and Sarigollu, 2012; Lin, 2013). Increased BA creates loyalty and long-term customer relationship opportunities for brands and consumers (Khan et al., 2015). BA plays a pivotal role in establishing recognition of fashion clothing brands among young consumers and positively affects brand equity (Sasmita and Mohd Suki, 2015).

The most recognized consumer purchase decision-making model presents five stages, where purchasing behavior differs from purchase intention (Engel et al., 1995). Morrison (1979) first distinguished between purchase intention and purchasing behavior: purchase intention reflects consumers’ willingness to buy a product, while purchasing behavior reflects whether consumers actually purchase what they previously intended to buy. Thus, purchasing behavior indicates actual buying behavior.

Much of the literature on BA has investigated the relationship between BA and purchase intention in fashion clothing contexts. However, relatively few studies have examined the link between BA and purchasing behavior. Consumers form behavior directly from situational or environmental circumstances, and the more they experience a product, the more awareness grows, stimulating purchasing behavior (Mowen and Minor, 2001; Ahn et al., 2018). For fashion clothing products, involvement positively influences fashion clothing knowledge or brand knowledge (O’Cass, 2004), thereby increasing BA.

Consumers with fashion BA often possess significant brand knowledge and emphasize specific fashion clothing brands by searching for and collecting information about them. This enables them to influence peers in promoting fashion clothing brands (Dahana et al., 2019). Conversely, high-level BA may be sufficient to influence decision-making in low-involvement situations because consumers rely on brand knowledge associated with BA (Hoyer and Brown, 1990; Siali, 2019).

**H1.** BA has a positive influence on MCPBFCB.

## Brand Consciousness and Brand Awareness

BC is the degree to which a consumer prefers to buy a popular brand’s product compared with other available brands (Sproles and Kendall, 1986). It also reflects the level of association consumers feel toward the product (Chu et al., 2019). Brand-conscious consumers prefer branded products and perceive brands as symbols of prestige and status (Liao and Wang, 2009; Khan et al., 2019).

Conscious consumers do not want to disengage from fashion trends because clothing communicates status and taste (Radhakrishnan, 2020). Kautish et al. (2020) found that terminal and instrumental values associated with luxury brands positively influence consumers’ BC, particularly for fashion apparel. Fashionable clothing brands enhance consumers’ self-image and motivate luxury consumption to impress others and establish high social status (Giovannini et al., 2015).

The proliferation of technology and online fashion retailers increases shopping convenience and positively affects consumers’ desire to revisit websites (Kautish and Rai, 2018), thereby enhancing BC. Fashion clothing brand shoppers exhibit high levels of BC (Sung and Jeon, 2009; Ladhari et al., 2019). BC for fashion clothing is pivotal for consumers who want to portray a prestigious and fashionable lifestyle (Ismail, 2017).

Prior research on logo changes found that only consumers with high BC put significant effort into processing new information because they valued changes in brand attributes (Van Grinsven and Das, 2016). Consumers are motivated to undertake this effort because of brand awareness from prior experience and the brand’s importance in their minds.

**H2.** BC has a positive influence on BA for fashion clothing brands.

## Brand Consciousness and Purchasing Behavior

Highly brand-conscious consumers seek quality products when making purchase decisions, particularly in clothing selection, where quality fit and brand name are important (Kaiser, 1998; Kim, 2008). Brand-conscious consumers tend to choose well-known and expensive options that represent them through styles rarely used by others (Musadik and Azmi, 2019).

Previous research has highlighted the positive influence of BC on consumer purchasing behavior (Gan et al., 2008). Brand-conscious consumers possess subjective preferences toward reputable brands; thus, their purchasing behavior differs from consumers with low BC. Brand-aware consumers are fashion-conscious and display risk aversion in purchase decisions through brand association (Liao and Wang, 2009). BC and quality consciousness are key drivers of online shopping behavior (Jadhav and Khanna, 2016).

BC reveals consumers’ underlying intention to purchase brands analogous to their self-image and identity and that display status and prestige (Giovannini et al., 2015). Sharda and Bhat (2018) found BC to be a significant predictor and driver of young consumers’ luxury brand consumption and purchase. Therefore, BC shapes purchase intention, which ultimately leads to actual purchasing behavior.

**H3.** BC has a positive influence on MCPBFCB.

## Brand Nationality, Brand Awareness and Purchasing Behavior

The proliferation of the Internet and globalization has brought information to consumers’ fingertips, enabling them to stay updated about fashion trends from different countries and celebrities. BN, also known as nation branding, refers to distinctive blends of elements with diversified dimensions that provide a nation with differentiation and association from a cultural perspective for target audiences (Dinnie, 2015).

Brands invest effort in crafting and positioning identity in consumers’ minds by familiarizing and inspiring consumers nationally and internationally (Mitterfellner, 2019). Nation branding activates connotations about a nation through what consumers experience, hear, and remember (Eusébio and Vieira, 2013). A nation’s brand is reflected through its image, values, identity, and communication to the external world, helping consumers of other nations build positive perceptions (Sataoen, 2019).

Country of origin, destination branding, country-product image, and country identity underpin nation branding (Fetscherin, 2010). This study focuses on country-product image. Country image influences evaluations of existing products, assessments of new products, and acceptance of foreign products (Tse and Gorn, 1993; Yang et al., 2018). Brands built on country image affect consumer product evaluations by evoking positive or negative feelings and ratings, leading to favorable or unfavorable product preferences (Semaan et al., 2019).

Consumers often perceive products from developed countries as better produced and manufactured (Lee et al., 2013). Brands associated with a perceived high-quality country image are already positioned and recognized in consumers’ minds, positively influencing risk-value perceptions and willingness to experience the product (Sharma, 2011). Because of BA and BN, consumers may favorably evaluate new products and brand line extensions from the same country.

**H4.** BN has a positive influence on BA of fashion clothing brands.

BN also positively influences consumer purchasing decisions because consumers believe a product’s or service’s origin signals quality. Some consumers have strong affinity toward foreign products, making this buying intention a priority for global marketers (Oberecker and Diamantopoulos, 2011). Nation branding with a favorable product-country image has an immediate effect on consumer beliefs and directly influences purchasing behavior (Fan, 2006; Yoon and Lee, 2019). Nation branding acts as an extrinsic cue for product evaluation and subsequent purchasing behavior (Coffey and Kabadayi, 2019).

**H5.** BN has a positive influence on MCPBFCB.

## Mediating Role of Brand Awareness

This study theorizes that BA exerts a mediated influence on purchasing behavior involving BC and BN. At the time of purchase, the first thing that comes to a consumer’s mind is the brand name, which reflects BA and brand recognition (Shabbir et al., 2010, 2017). Despite the direct effects of BC and BN on purchasing behavior, BA is expected to transmit the effects of BC and BN indirectly.

Previous research has examined the mediating role of BA between consumers’ emotional dimensions and purchase intentions for high-involvement products (ShahinSharifi, 2014), between marketing-mix elements and consumer satisfaction (Garg et al., 2016), between brand advertisement and brand commitment in female apparel (Khan et al., 2016), and between corporate social responsibility and brand equity (Hafez, 2018). However, the mediating role of BA between BC and purchasing behavior, and between BN and purchasing behavior, is rarely explored in the context of fashion clothing brands.

**H6.** BA mediates the relationship between BC and MCPBFCB.

**H7.** BA mediates the relationship between BN and MCPBFCB.

## Brand Schematicity

Branding has a significant impact on understanding consumer behavior. Consumer–brand relationships have been extensively researched in marketing, with findings showing that some consumers respond similarly to firms’ branding activities, whereas others are either brand-schematic or brand-aschematic (Puligadda et al., 2012).

BS is an individual characteristic indicating that brand-schematic consumers process product information with reference to available brand information, while brand-aschematic consumers consider other details instead (Puligadda et al., 2012). Commercially positioned brands are prioritized by brand-schematic consumers, and BS can be applied across product categories (Carsana and Jolibert, 2017). Brand-schematic consumers organize, interpret, and retrieve consumption-related information at the brand-node level (Keller, 1998; Kim et al., 2019). They value brands that are important to them.

Brand information is perceived diagnostically by brand-schematic consumers, who adopt schema-related dimensions to evaluate brand-related news (Meyers-Levy and Tybout, 1989; Puligadda et al., 2012). Prior research has examined the moderating role of BS in aesthetic brands and brand accessories (Jeon and Lee, 2016), product cues in purchasing situations (Carsana and Jolibert, 2017), and perceived authenticity of private-label brands (Carsana and Jolibert, 2018).

In the fashion clothing brand context, consumers process extensive information in brand-associated settings: BN, BC, BA, and MCPBFCB. Consumers who are receptive deliberately receive, store, interpret, and retrieve brand information for decision-making. Therefore, individual differences in brand information processing reflect the impact of branding efforts, purchasing behavior, and broader consumer behavior.

**H8.** The higher consumers’ BS, the higher the positive effect of BC on MCPBFCB.

**H9.** The higher consumers’ BS, the higher the positive effect of BA on MCPBFCB.

**H10.** The higher consumers’ BS, the higher the positive effect of BN on MCPBFCB.

## Research Methods

The study used structural equation modeling (SEM) to analyze the proposed conceptual model because SEM is flexible regarding data requirements, model complexity, and relationship specifications. AMOS 20 and SPSS 20 were used for statistical analysis. Before hypothesis testing, the survey data were verified for reliability and validity.

The study adopted Cronbach’s alpha and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess instrument reliability and robustness. Composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), and factor loadings were also used to test reliability and validity (Hair et al., 2010).

## Data Collection and Sample Profile

The study used self-administered questionnaires through shopping mall and university campus intercept procedures, similar to Hossain et al. (2017). A convenience sampling process was adopted to generate a substantial number of respondents. The sample consisted of millennial consumers in Bangladesh born between 1980 and 2000, aged 19–39 years at the time of data collection.

Data were collected over a 14-week period, including weekdays and weekends. The study measured millennial consumers’ purchase behavior toward fashion clothing brands in shopping-related and university campus environments. During data collection, snowball sampling was also applied, using subjects to identify other eligible participants aged 19–39. Trained research assistants collected the data.

The survey was designed to take no more than 15 minutes to complete. It began with screening questions to verify respondent eligibility. Constructs were then measured, including BC, BA, BN, and BS as possible predictors of MCPBFCB. The survey ended with demographic questions. Each construct was measured using a five-point Likert scale, with 5 representing strongly agree and 1 representing strongly disagree.

## Construct Operationalization and Data Analysis Method

The survey instruments were constructed based on validated scales from previous studies. Constructs were measured using multi-item scales. To investigate MCPBFCB, the study adapted the following existing scales:

- BC: six items adapted from Giovannini et al. (2015)
- BN: six items adapted from MohdYasin et al. (2007)
- MCPBFCB: three items adapted from Caruana (2002)
- BA: four items adapted from Yoo and Donthu (2001)
- BS: six items adapted from Zarantonello and Pauwels-Delassus (2015)

## Common Method Variance

Common method bias in SEM-oriented research can arise from the estimation technique used rather than from the causal system in the model. For example, instructions at the beginning of an instrument may influence responses in a similar direction, causing indicators to share common variation.

The study addressed common method variance both through research design and statistical testing. In the research design, scale items were improved by eliminating ambiguity, reducing social desirability bias, carefully selecting words, and using positive and negative elements. The study defined unfamiliar terms, avoided vague concepts, clarified concepts to respondents, kept the instrument simple, specific, and concise, and avoided double-barreled questions.

Harman’s single-factor test was applied using exploratory factor analysis for all datasets. The results showed that the single-factor solution accounted for only 33.05% of total variance. Therefore, common method variance was not a concern, and the study proceeded to CFA and hypothesis testing.

## Research Findings

### CFA and Assessment of Validity and Reliability

CFA was performed on five constructs: BC, BN, MCPBFCB, BA, and BS. Maximum likelihood estimation in AMOS was used before incorporating structural relationships (Jöreskog and Sörbom, 1993). The measurement model was assessed using unidimensionality, reliability, validity, and normality.

Data achieve unidimensionality when all items under each construct have acceptable factor loadings greater than 0.5 (Hair et al., 2006). Reliability was examined using Cronbach’s alpha, with values of 0.7 or higher considered acceptable (Nunnally and Bernstein, 1994). Convergent and discriminant validity were examined for each construct.

For convergent validity, CR must be at least 0.7 and AVE must be at least 0.5 (Hair et al., 2010). The data fulfilled these requirements. Discriminant validity was tested using the square root of AVE for each construct, which should exceed correlation coefficients among constructs. The statistical results met this threshold.

The CFA results suggested a satisfactory model fit. Additional fit measures, including GFI, AGFI, CFI, TLI, and RMSEA, also indicated that the data fit the proposed model. All standardized factor loadings were significant at the 0.001 level, supporting convergent validity. CR, AVE, Cronbach’s alpha, maximum shared variance (MSV), and average shared variance (ASV) were calculated. The results indicated that the measurement model was valid and reliable.

## Structural Model

After confirming validity and reliability, SEM was used to test the hypothesized relationships among BA, BC, BN, BS, and MCPBFCB. The model fit was satisfactory, and the fit indices met suggested cut-off values.

## Hypotheses Testing

Each hypothesized relationship was examined using path significance tests. The results showed that BA significantly influenced MCPBFCB, supporting H1. BC significantly influenced BA, supporting H2. BC also had a positive influence on MCPBFCB, supporting H3. BN significantly influenced BA, supporting H4. BN also had a significant positive influence on MCPBFCB, supporting H5.

To test the mediating effect of BA between BC and MCPBFCB, as well as between BN and MCPBFCB, the study applied the procedure recommended by Baron and Kenny (1986). A variable functions as a mediator when:

1. The independent variables significantly influence the mediating variable.
2. The mediating variable significantly affects the dependent variable.
3. When the mediator is controlled, the previously significant relationship between independent and dependent variables becomes insignificant.

The results showed that both BC and BN significantly influenced BA. BA also significantly influenced MCPBFCB. After introducing BA as a mediator, the direct paths became insignificant, indicating full mediation. BA demonstrated a stronger mediating effect between BC and MCPBFCB than between BN and MCPBFCB. Thus, H6 and H7 were supported.

The moderating role of BS was also examined. Significant interaction effects were found among the tested relationships. The interaction between BC and BS was significant, and simple slope analysis showed that BC was positively related to MCPBFCB when millennials perceived a high level of BS. Thus, H8 was supported.

The interaction between BS and BA was also significant, indicating that the higher consumers’ BS, the higher the positive effect of BA on MCPBFCB. Thus, H9 was supported. Similarly, BN was positively related to MCPBFCB when BS was high, supporting H10.

Overall, the SEM results revealed that the proposed conceptual model predicts millennials’ purchasing behavior in light of BC, BN, BA, and BS.

## Discussion

Millennials’ purchasing behavior has been studied extensively across product and service segments such as cosmetics, apparel, technology, and tourism. Different generations display distinct behavioral dimensions, making millennials an interesting group to study because of their unique characteristics and high BA (Moreno et al., 2017).

The existing body of research has demonstrated consumer behavior in association with BA, BC, and BN. Analyzing MCPBFCB can help marketers and designers understand consumer needs and desires regarding fashion clothing brands and help them reform brand-related marketing strategies.

This study tested whether BC, BN, and BA foster MCPBFCB. It also investigated direct and indirect links between millennial consumers’ BC and BN, as well as the mediating role of BA. The findings support the effect of BA and show how it mediates relationships. Specifically, BA reconciles the paths from BC and BN to MCPBFCB.

The SEM results indicate that MCPBFCB is not merely a matter of quality, promotion, pricing, and distribution. Rather, BC, BN, and overall BA among millennials play important roles. BA actively mediates the relationship between BC and MCPBFCB, as well as between BN and MCPBFCB. This supports previous research arguing that consumers are conscious of brands before making repeat purchases.

The findings also show that BA is an influencing factor between BC and millennial consumers’ fashion clothing brand purchasing behavior in Bangladesh’s competitive fashion clothing market. The study also investigated the moderating role of BS in the relationships between BC and MCPBFCB, BN and MCPBFCB, and BA and MCPBFCB. All MCPBFCB variables were significantly influenced by BS. For individuals with low BS, BC, BA, and BN had no effect on MCPBFCB. The interaction effect remained significant after controlling for demographics, including gender.

## Theoretical Implications

Marketing and consumer buying behavior are closely related, making the influence of branding variables on actual buying behavior important. This study contributes to understanding millennials, a profitable segment for fashion retailers.

Cognitive schema theory shows how knowledge about a particular fashion brand is formed and conceptualized in the target customer’s mind when deciding to buy. Although prior research has applied this theory only limitedly, it is significant because it explains how consumers process information and make buying decisions. The inner structure of schema remains stable across contexts, enabling researchers to treat schematic properties as important parts of a theoretical framework that can be applied in various settings (Halkias, 2015).

The study empirically contributes to the literature on millennial purchasing behavior through cognitive self-schema theory (Samala and Singh, 2019; Segal, 1988). Its uniqueness lies in examining BA, BS, BC, and BN under the cognitive schema theory paradigm. It is also one of the first efforts in the context of Bangladeshi millennial consumers to identify antecedents such as BA and BS that change the relationship between BC, BN, and MCPBFCB.

Previous research has emphasized experience, perceptual maps, consumer status, brand engagement, brand involvement, and brand knowledge in millennial purchasing behavior. This study contributes to brand management and consumer research by demonstrating that MCPBFCB is significantly influenced by BC and BN, with BA as a mediator and BS as a moderator among direct relationships.

## Managerial Implications

From a managerial perspective, this research explains MCPBFCB through BA, BC, BN, and BS. The study confirms that fashion clothing brand marketers should use accessible social media technologies such as Facebook and Twitter to create BA and brand preference among millennials, establish future preferences, and possibly build brand loyalty.

Marketers should use both online and offline advertising to develop BA for fashion clothing brands, as millennials are a niche but attractive market segment. To influence purchasing behavior, marketers need to understand trends that clarify millennials’ sensitivity and awareness toward local and international fashion clothing brands.

Millennials are growing up in an environment where technology provides personalization and immediate gratification. Therefore, marketers should continuously update online content and train managers to use social network sites effectively to respond quickly to millennial demands. The results also support the idea that millennials are brand-conscious and display significant brand loyalty toward fashion clothing brands. Millennials with high BC are more concerned about BA information when forming purchasing behavior.

The role of BS is also important. Millennial shoppers with high BS are more attentive to brand data and can be reached through online and offline advertising that emphasizes fashion clothing brands rather than product attributes. In contrast, brand-aschematic purchasers are likely to be persuaded more by product attribute-oriented information.

BS can speed information processing and influence judgment regarding BC, BA, and BN. It gives consumers an accessible way to choose among fashion clothing brand alternatives. The study shows that information is vital to millennials when selecting the right fashion clothing brand.

Fashion clothing brand managers can focus on brand-schematic cues by providing consumers with information such as leaflets explaining a brand’s origin and value. Consumers should also be offered technology-mediated tools to scan data about rational cues before choosing a fashion brand. A brand-schematic buyer may be motivated to find and store information about brands used by reference groups or use brand patterns to form group preferences. Therefore, BS can serve as an active retail strategy with important implications for MCPBFCB.

Because fashion clothing products are directly connected to brand perception, advertisers should concentrate on brand-related cues to attract millennials. Consumers with higher BS display stronger concern for BC, BA, and BN. Marketers should improve consumers’ knowledge of fashion clothing items by distributing important brand-related information through well-known media. To strengthen relationships with millennial consumers, marketers should use customized data, promotions, and referral programs to build BC and BA and increase knowledge of national and international brands.

## Limitations of the Study and Future Research

The findings contribute to consumer research in the millennial segment and to fashion clothing brand literature. However, several limitations exist. The first limitation concerns the sample and scope of the investigation. Although the current sample is statistically justified, it may not represent the entire population. A longer data collection period and a larger sample could improve representativeness and generalizability.

The study did not consider factors such as consumer feelings, emotions, customer service, store condition, and retailer qualities. Future research may examine these aspects.

The proposed model was developed to study how millennials’ purchasing behavior is influenced by BC, BA, BN, and BS. Future research may include brand experience and brand-related stimuli to understand MCPBFCB more comprehensively. The current study was also limited to one product category: fashion clothing brands. Further research into other product categories, such as luxury products, destination tourism, or services, could add meaningful findings to brand literature.
