# Measuring the Impact of Beliefs on Luxury Buying Behavior in an Emerging Market

## Empirical Evidence from India

## Abstract

**Purpose** – Demand for luxury brands is increasing at a very fast pace in emerging markets like India. However, very few quantitative studies have been conducted to explore the reasons behind this sudden surge in demand. Thus, the purpose of this paper is to understand the impact of beliefs on consumer buying behavior for luxury fashion brands in the Indian context, employing the theory of planned behavior, and to develop a comprehensive understanding of the motivating factors behind luxury goods consumption.

**Design/methodology/approach** – Researcher-controlled sampling techniques, including judgment and snowball sampling, were employed to collect data from actual users of luxury fashion brands in New Delhi, India. Statistical tests, including confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modeling, were applied for data analysis.

**Findings** – The findings show that all three beliefs—attitudinal belief, normative belief, and control belief—were positively and significantly related to attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control. Normative belief was found to have a positive impact on perceived behavioral control as well as actual consumer purchasing behavior for luxury fashion brands.

**Originality/value** – This research contributes to the extant literature by bringing to light new findings that could help provide meaningful insights to academicians and marketing practitioners.

**Keywords:** India, brands, consumer behaviour, fashion, luxury, theory of planned behaviour

**Paper type:** Research paper

## Introduction

The overall personal luxury goods market grew to around €250 billion in 2015 at the rate of 13 percent, while real growth slipped to 1-2 percent on account of the global economic slowdown (Bain & Company, 2015). Although the worldwide luxury goods market is sluggish, the Indian luxury market has been rapidly growing at 25 percent per annum and is estimated to reach $18.3 million (Chaturvedi, 2016). In fact, India is among the fastest-growing luxury markets across the globe (Aroche, 2015).

Key factors leading to a surge in demand for luxury brands in India include the burgeoning urban middle-class population, which has a penchant for global luxury brands; the growing number of ultra-high-net-worth individuals (HNIs), those with a net worth exceeding $50 million; and the rise in the number of dollar millionaires, who are estimated to grow by 65 percent from 185,000 in 2015 to 305,000 by 2020 (Credit Suisse Global Wealth Report, 2015).

Although the luxury market in India is in a nascent stage of development and contributes only 1-2 percent of the global luxury market (Euromonitor International, 2016), it remains very lucrative for international luxury players and provides huge business opportunities for growth and expansion. Apart from the traditional rich, who are regular buyers, and rich professionals, who are conscious spenders, there is a large untapped segment comprising businessmen with significant resources but who do not yet have an eye to appreciate fine luxury brands due to lack of exposure, accessibility, and brand awareness. This group is expected to soon become the largest consumer segment for luxury goods in India, translating into a monumental increase in demand for luxury brands.

Various studies have been conducted to understand luxury purchase behavior in the Western context (Vigneron and Johnson, 2004; O’Cass and McEwen, 2004; Tsai, 2005; Summers et al., 2006; Wiedmann et al., 2007; Amatulli and Guido, 2011; Cheah et al., 2015). However, little effort has been made by researchers and marketers to understand luxury consumer behavior in the context of emerging economies such as India, due to the historically insignificant presence of luxury brands in these markets.

In recent times, with increased internet penetration, a rise in the number of HNIs, affluent millennials, informed consumers, independent youth, wider accessibility of luxury brands, mushrooming upper-middle-class population, and increased purchasing power of consumers, the Indian luxury market has become very attractive for global luxury companies. Thus, a need existed to carry out a comprehensive study to systematically understand and analyze the key factors that affect purchase behavior of Indian consumers with respect to luxury fashion brands.

This study is organized as follows: it presents a review of literature followed by the conceptual framework and hypotheses development. The next section provides the research methodology, highlighting data collection and sampling techniques. The following section presents key findings. Toward the end, implications and directions for future research are discussed.

## Literature Review and Hypotheses Development

The word “luxury” is derived from the Latin word *luxuria*, which means “extras of life” (Danziger, 2005). There is no universally recognized marketing definition of luxury goods in the literature (Dubois and Laurent, 1996; Vickers and Renand, 2003; Beverland, 2004). Many authors have worked in this area (Leibenstein, 1950; Vigneron and Johnson, 2004; Wiedmann et al., 2007).

Smith (1776) classified consumption into four groups: “necessary” goods, which sustain life; “basic” goods, which support normal growth and well-being; “affluence” goods, which are not necessary for growth and prosperity; and “luxury” goods, which are scarce, difficult to attain, and/or very expensive. Different people may perceive different things as luxury (Hauck and Stanforth, 2007).

According to Kapferer (1997), “luxury defines beauty; it is art applied to functional items,” and luxury items “provide extra pleasure and flatter all senses at once.” In the literature, researchers have often used “luxury” to describe the most prestigious brands (Vigneron and Johnson, 2004). Consumers achieve supremacy and prominence by consuming these goods (Cheah et al., 2015), a phenomenon known as “conspicuous consumption” (O’Cass and McEwen, 2004). Luxury brands bestow respect and satisfy their owners’ hedonic and utilitarian wants (Choo et al., 2012).

## Factors Behind Luxury Goods Consumption

Studies in luxury consumer behavior reflect that both social and personal orientations result in the consumption of luxury goods (Wong and Ahuvia, 1998; Vigneron and Johnson, 1999, 2004; Vickers and Renand, 2003; Tsai, 2005; Jain et al., 2015). The acquisition of luxury goods brings esteem to the user in addition to providing functional value (Shukla, 2010; Vigneron and Johnson, 2004).

Luxury goods consumption is motivated by interpersonal effects, namely the Veblen, snob, and bandwagon effects, and personal effects, namely hedonic and perfectionism effects (Vigneron and Johnson, 1999). Jain et al. (2015), in their conceptual study, concluded that cultural orientation, intrinsic factors, extrinsic factors, and resources lead to luxury brand purchase intention. Zhang and Kim (2013) revealed that brand consciousness, social comparison, and fashion innovativeness have a deep influence on Chinese consumers’ luxury goods consumption. However, a qualitative study by Amatulli and Guido (2010) observed that inner motivation and quality value are the key determinants of luxury purchase intention in the Italian market. Park et al. (2007) found that materialism, conformity, need for uniqueness, and social recognition drive luxury purchase intention among Korean consumers.

## Theory of Reasoned Action and Theory of Planned Behavior

The theoretical foundations of the present study rest on the theory of reasoned action suggested by Fishbein and Ajzen (1975) and Ajzen and Fishbein (1980), as well as the theory of planned behavior proposed by Ajzen (1991). Ajzen (1991) added the perceived behavioral control variable to the existing two variables—attitude toward behavior and subjective norm—in order to deal with non-volitional situations.

According to the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991), human behavior is influenced by three beliefs: attitudinal beliefs, normative beliefs, and control beliefs. Attitudinal beliefs cause favorable or unfavorable attitude toward the behavior, normative beliefs produce perceived social pressures, and control beliefs cause perceived behavioral control. In turn, attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control lead to behavioral intention, which further results in actual purchase behavior.

The theory of planned behavior posits that an individual intends to perform a behavior when they evaluate it positively, experience social pressure to perform it, and believe they have the means to do it (Ajzen, 1988, 1991). These theories have played a crucial role in providing a deep understanding of both personal and social factors when applied to various consumer behavior studies (Lee and Green, 1991; Chung and Pysarchik, 2000; Ling, 2009; Sanyal et al., 2014).

## Attitudinal Beliefs

Attitudes are cognitive, affective, and behavioral. The cognitive base for attitude formation assumes that beliefs and attitudes are formed through the process of reasoning. In contrast, affective or behavioral bases for attitude formation emphasize stimulus-response mechanisms (Underwood, 2008). A person’s attitude represents their personal convictions and feelings toward the behavior of interest (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975).

An individual who believes that performing a specific behavior will result in a positive outcome will have a favorable attitude toward performing that behavior. The beliefs underlying attitude toward the behavior are termed “attitudinal beliefs.” In other words, beliefs about the presence of factors that may facilitate or impede performance of behavior are referred to as “behavioral beliefs” or “attitudinal beliefs” (Sommer, 2011).

According to the theory of reasoned action (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980), attitude takes into consideration beliefs about the consequences of performing the behavior and the evaluation of these consequences:

```plain text
Aact = Σ BiEi
```


Various studies have confirmed that cognitive belief is a significant predictor of attitude (Miniard and Cohen, 1983; Lee and Green, 1991; Netemeyer and Bearden, 1992). Attitude toward luxury brands has an important influence on buyers’ intentions to purchase luxury brands (Bian, 2010; Zhang and Kim, 2013). Yoo and Lee (2009) found that consumers’ positive attitude toward purchasing counterfeits has a significant impact on purchase intention. Several other researchers have indicated that behavioral intention is directly related to attitude in the consumer decision-making process (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980; Shen et al., 2003; Fitzmaurice, 2005; Summers et al., 2006; Bellman et al., 2009).

Hence, the hypotheses posited in this study are as follows:

**H1.** Attitudinal belief has a significant, direct, and positive impact on attitude in relation to the purchase of luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

**H2.** Attitude has a significant, direct, and positive impact on purchase intention for luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

## Normative Beliefs

Subjective norms are the individual’s perception of social pressures for or against performing a behavior (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975). Subjective norms may put pressure regarding performance or non-performance of the behavior in question, independent of an individual’s own attitude toward the behavior. The different influencers involved in subjective norms may include family, friends, and colleagues.

The beliefs underlying subjective norms are known as “normative beliefs.” They help in the prediction of subjective norms, intention, and behavior. Subjective norms are examined by normative beliefs and motivation to comply with those beliefs (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980):

```plain text
SN = Σ NBjMCj
```


Subjective norm has been found to be positively related to Chinese consumers’ luxury purchase intention (Ling, 2009). Various other studies have shown a significant relationship between subjective norm and purchase intention (Teo and Pok, 2003; Ramayah et al., 2003, 2004; Yulihasri, 2004; Chan and Lu, 2004; May, 2005; Kim and Karpova, 2010).

Hence, the hypotheses posited in this study are as follows:

**H3.** Normative belief has a significant, direct, and positive impact on subjective norm in relation to the purchase of luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

**H4.** Subjective norm has a significant, direct, and positive impact on purchase intention for luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

## Control Belief

Perceived behavioral control is defined as “an individual’s perception of the ease or difficulty in performing the behavior of interest, given the presence or absence of requisite resources and opportunities” (Ajzen, 1991). Perceived behavioral control is evaluated by the total set of control beliefs, that is, beliefs about the existence of factors that may promote or hinder performance of behavior (Ajzen, 1991). Particularly, the influence of each control belief is weighted by the perceived power of the control factor:

```plain text
PBC = Σ CiPi
```


Ling (2009) concluded that perceived behavioral control is a key factor in determining purchase intention of luxury consumers in China. Numerous studies have confirmed that perceived behavioral control is a predictor of purchase intention (Kim and Karpova, 2010; Jin and Kang, 2011; Son and Jin, 2013).

Hence, the hypotheses posited in this study are as follows:

**H5.** Control belief has a significant, direct, and positive impact on perceived behavioral control in relation to the purchase of luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

**H6a.** Perceived behavioral control has a significant, direct, and positive impact on purchase intention for luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

**H6b.** Perceived behavioral control has a significant, direct, and positive impact on purchase of luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

## Behavioral Intention

Behavioral intention is defined as “the perception of an individual toward performance of a particular behavior” (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975). When behavior is under a person’s control, intention can predict actual behavior with significant accuracy (Ajzen, 1988). However, intention and behavior may not be perfectly correlated (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975).

There is a strong bias toward overestimating the probability of doing desired behavior and underestimating the probability of not doing undesired behavior. This may result in variability between intention and actual behavior (Ajzen et al., 2004). In cases where the time interval between intention and behavior is short, they may be highly correlated (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1981).

Past studies have found purchase intention to be an antecedent of actual purchase behavior (Zeithaml et al., 1996; Bian and Forsythe, 2012; Li et al., 2012; Zhang and Kim, 2013).

Hence, the hypothesis posited in this study is as follows:

**H7.** Purchase intention has a significant, direct, and positive impact on purchase of luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

## Conceptual Framework

The growing demand for luxury has made it imperative for luxury brand managers and academicians to understand the motivators behind luxury goods consumption in the Indian context. So far, no quantitative study has been conducted to understand the impact of beliefs on consumer purchase behavior for luxury fashion brands in India, specifically employing the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991).

This study is an effort to address this gap in the literature. It tries to determine the applicability and validity of the theory of planned behavior in explaining consumer behavior with respect to the purchase of luxury fashion brands in an emerging market such as India. It also suggests a behavioral framework to help practitioners and marketers better understand, analyze, and explain consumer buying behavior for luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

## Methodology and Measurement

### Data Collection

Based on an extensive review of literature (Phau and Leng, 2008; Ling, 2009; Hung et al., 2011; Shukla, 2011; Kumar, 2012) and practical insights, the survey method was considered the most appropriate for data collection in the present study. Further, the survey method is best suited to understand the relationship between variables such as beliefs, attitude, and purchase intention (Bryman, 2012).

The study questionnaire was divided into two sections. Section A comprised screening questions as well as questions pertaining to beliefs, attitude, intention, and other constructs considered in the theory of planned behavior model. Section B was designed to generate the demographic profile of subjects. As recommended in previous studies, a screening question was employed to identify actual users of luxury fashion brands by asking them to share names of luxury fashion brands owned by them (Christodoulides et al., 2008; Shukla, 2010, 2011).

The researcher-controlled sampling technique used in the present study has been employed in numerous extant studies in the luxury domain, giving valid and reliable results (Park et al., 2007; Gao et al., 2009; Zhan and He, 2012). A total of 350 questionnaires were administered, of which 140 valid responses were obtained through mall intercept surveys, while 117 responses were generated using the online version of the instrument. A t-test yielded insignificant differences between offline and online responses. Thus, the data obtained through these two modes of administration were similar and responses could be analyzed collectively.

Very few studies in the past have used actual luxury brand consumers as study samples (e.g. Summers et al., 2006; Ling, 2009; Ross and Harradine, 2011). Personal interviews were conducted with patrons of luxury who were enrolled in various programs at a leading fashion institute in New Delhi, India. Data were also generated from actual users of luxury brands visiting a high-end mall in New Delhi, a popular destination for luxury patrons.

An online version of the questionnaire was developed and hosted on Google Docs. Patrons of luxury brands were approached while shopping and requested to provide feedback using the paper-and-pencil version of the questionnaire. Owing to a paucity of time, some respondents expressed inability to respond immediately but promised to provide feedback to the online version of the instrument. Willing patrons of luxury consented to share their e-mail IDs, and subsequently the online questionnaire link was e-mailed to them.

In addition, some respondents were persuaded to share contact details of family members and friends who regularly shopped for luxury fashion brands. These individuals were later contacted via e-mail through referrals and sent the online questionnaire link. These measures helped generate quality responses from actual patrons of luxury fashion brands.

Of the total 257 respondents, around 35 percent were male and 65 percent were female. The majority of respondents were young, ranging between 26 and 35 years of age. Postgraduates constituted around 60 percent of the sample, and around three-fourths of the respondents had an annual income greater than US$75,000. Therefore, it can be safely concluded that educated affluent millennials are the major buyers of luxury fashion brands in India. These results are in line with earlier studies (Zhan and He, 2012; Li et al., 2011; Hung et al., 2011).

### Scale Development

Measurement scales for this study were developed in line with suggestions by Francis et al. (2004), Ajzen (2007), and inputs from earlier studies on the subject (e.g. Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980; Son, 2007; Ling, 2009). First, face validity and content validity were assessed. Then a pilot study was conducted, and scales were refined based on expert suggestions and respondent feedback. Thereafter, final data were collected from actual buyers of luxury fashion brands.

For all variables other than attitude and perceived behavioral control, a five-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (+1) to strongly agree (+5) was used. Purchase behavior was measured using scales developed by Schlegelmilch et al. (1996) and Dubois et al. (2005). An additional item, “I would mostly buy luxury goods for myself,” was included based on suggestions from the expert panel.

Purchase intention was measured using scales developed by Madden et al. (1992), Ajzen and Fishbein (1980), and Bian and Forsythe (2012). Attitude was measured by three items generated from studies by Ling (2009), Fitzmaurice (2005), and Madden et al. (1992), using a seven-point semantic differential scale. Subjective norm was measured by four items generated from studies by Ajzen and Fishbein (1980), Ling (2009), and Fitzmaurice (2005). The scale to measure perceived behavioral control comprised four items borrowed from Francis et al. (2004) and Shim et al. (2001); two items were assessed on a five-point Likert scale and the remaining items were analyzed using a seven-point semantic differential scale.

In the present research, all five motivations of luxury goods consumption—quality value, social value, conspicuous value, hedonic value, and uniqueness value—as prescribed by Vigneron and Johnson (1999), were examined to measure attitudinal beliefs. Conspicuous value was measured using items from Truong et al. (2008) and Ling (2009). Items related to uniqueness value were derived from Ling (2009) and Tian et al. (2001). Social value was measured using items from Li et al. (2011) and Smith and Colgate (2007). Hedonic value items were adopted from scales suggested by Spangenberg et al. (1997), Tsai (2005), Smith and Colgate (2007), and Babin et al. (1994). Quality value was measured by items developed by Tsai (2005) and Mathwick et al. (2001).

The scale items for measuring normative beliefs and motivation to comply were adopted from Ajzen and Fishbein (1980). Three items each for measuring control beliefs and perceived power suggested by Ling (2009) were also included in the research instrument.

## Results

### Measurement Model, Reliability, and Validity

Confirmatory factor analysis was performed to test the measurement model. The results showed acceptable fit to the data, with χ² = 489.74, degrees of freedom = 223, normed χ² = 2.196, AGFI = 0.811, CFI = 0.903, IFI = 0.905, and RMSEA = 0.068. All values were within the acceptable range recommended by Hair et al. (2010).

The convergent validity of the measurement model was tested by assessing the statistical significance of factor loadings, average variance extracted, and construct reliability (Hair et al., 2010). After deleting two items from attitudinal belief and one item each from subjective norm, perceived behavioral control, and purchase behavior, the overall fit statistics of the measurement model containing 24 manifest indicators for eight latent constructs were within the acceptable range suggested by Fornell and Larcker (1981) and Hair et al. (2010).

To check discriminant validity, values of average variance extracted and squared correlations of constructs were compared for each pair of constructs. This criterion was met, as no correlation exceeded the square root of the average variance extracted.

### Structural Model Test

Structural equation modeling was applied to test the conceptual model and proposed hypotheses using AMOS version 22 and maximum likelihood estimates. The result of the initial structural equation modeling test did not produce a good model fit to the data. Some values were not within the acceptable range recommended by Hair et al. (2010).

The modified theory of planned behavior model revealed good model fit, as all values were within the acceptable range suggested by Hair et al. (2010): normed χ² = 2.145, IFI = 0.903, AGFI = 0.814, CFI = 0.902, and RMSEA = 0.067. Based on modification indices, the following paths were added: a directed path from normative belief to purchase behavior, a directed path from normative belief to perceived behavioral control, and a directed path from attitudinal beliefs to subjective norm. These relationships were also observed in other studies (Ling, 2009; Oliver and Bearden, 1985).

Attitudinal belief had a positive and significant association with attitude toward luxury behavior and subjective norm. Normative belief was positively and significantly related to subjective norm, perceived behavioral control, and purchase behavior. Control belief also showed a positive and significant relationship with perceived behavioral control. Attitude and subjective norm both showed a significant and positive impact on luxury purchase intention.

In contrast, a statistically significant relationship did not emerge between perceived behavioral control and purchase intention. However, perceived behavioral control was positively and significantly related to actual purchase behavior. Purchase intention was found to have a positive but insignificant relationship with purchase behavior.

The modified model resulted in three additional hypotheses:

**H8.** Attitudinal belief has a significant, direct, and positive impact on subjective norm in relation to the purchase of luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

**H9.** Normative belief has a significant, direct, and positive impact on perceived behavioral control in relation to the purchase of luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

**H10.** Normative belief has a significant, direct, and positive impact on actual purchase behavior toward luxury fashion brands by consumers in India.

The data supported all three additional hypotheses.

## Discussion and Findings

This study provides a comprehensive understanding of the impact of beliefs on Indian consumers’ purchasing behavior for luxury fashion brands. The study revealed that the theory of planned behavior model suggested by Ajzen (1991) did not satisfactorily explain consumer behavior with respect to luxury fashion brands in the context of the Indian market. However, when the model was modified based on statistical inferences and suggestions from earlier researchers such as Ling (2009), it successfully predicted purchase behavior toward luxury brands among Indian consumers.

The results obtained through structural equation modeling confirmed that all three beliefs—attitudinal belief, normative belief, and control belief—were positively and significantly related to attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control. These results were in line with the theory of planned behavior model and similar results obtained in earlier studies (Ajzen, 1991; Ling, 2009). Results indicate that positive attitudinal belief toward purchasing luxury goods leads to a positive attitude toward purchasing luxury goods. Similarly, social support from friends, family, and colleagues regarding the purchase of luxury goods leads to a positive subjective norm. The results also indicate that favorable control belief leads to positive perceived behavioral control.

Attitudinal belief about purchasing luxury fashion goods was found to be an antecedent not only of attitude but also of subjective norm. Normative belief showed a positive and significant relationship with perceived behavioral control. These findings suggest that individuals backed by social support from friends, family, and colleagues perceive greater control over purchasing luxury fashion goods. These results are consistent with earlier studies (Ling, 2009).

The present research also revealed that normative belief can have a direct influence on actual purchase behavior, signifying that consumers with social support can directly decide to buy luxury goods without the mediating role of subjective norm and purchase intention. Thus, it can be concluded that “significant others” play an important role in luxury buying behavior among Indian consumers. This can have a direct bearing on luxury goods purchase. These results find support in earlier research showing that collectivists emphasize group affiliation and public reputation (Wong and Ahuvia, 1998).

Results of this study reflect that both attitude and subjective norm are direct predictors of purchase intention. These results align with various past studies (Kloeblen and Thompson, 1999; Choo et al., 2004; Ramayah et al., 2004; Ling, 2009). In contrast, a weak relationship was found between perceived behavioral control and purchase intention. Similar results emerged in other studies (Ma, 2007).

Perceived behavioral control was found to have a direct, positive, and significant relationship with actual purchase behavior, showing that consumers with resources and opportunities may directly buy luxury fashion brands without the mediating role of intent. These results are supported by previous studies by Ajzen (1988) and Madden et al. (1992).

Few studies in the past have concluded that purchase intention may not accurately predict purchase behavior (Albrecht and Carpenter, 1976; Ajzen et al., 2004; Son, 2007; Sanyal et al., 2014). This study also found an insignificant relationship between purchase intention and purchase behavior. This reflects intent to buy luxury brands among Indian consumers; however, since these brands are exorbitantly priced, consumers may not be able to actually purchase them.

## Implications

### Practical Implications

This study provides valuable implications for international luxury brands wishing to penetrate and expand in the Indian market. It helps explore the unique luxury buying behavior of affluent Indian consumers and the driving force behind their purchase decisions. The modified theory of planned behavior model suggested in this study provides new and deeper insights. It reveals that normative belief has a direct impact on Indian consumers’ actual purchase behavior toward luxury goods.

Social support from friends, family, and colleagues was found to be a major trigger leading to the purchase of luxury brands by Indians. Further, India being a collectivist society, reference groups play a critical role in the consumer decision-making process. This finding has significant implications for marketers. By emphasizing the importance of reference groups in advertising campaigns, marketers can convey the influence of normative beliefs and subjective norms on the target group.

Marketing managers can segment the luxury market based on social, personal, psychographic, and behavioral variables and target consumers effectively depending on the values they seek in luxury brands, such as quality value, status value, hedonic value, materialistic value, and self-concept value. Varied communication strategies need to be devised for different target groups.

Marketers can decide on strategies that embrace the fact that positive word of mouth can have a viral effect on Indian consumers. The use of interpersonal channels can result in significant gains for luxury companies. To convert potential customers into actual buyers, marketers need to develop favorable attitudes toward luxury brands through promotional activity in fashion events, exhibitions, fashion magazines, luxury malls, and similar platforms.

Marketers can also use appropriate online platforms dealing in luxury products, such as blogs, Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube, to promote these brands because of the growing acceptance of these communication channels among affluent consumers in India (Jain et al., 2014). In India, Bollywood stars also have a deep impact on consumers’ mindsets (Chadha and Husband, 2006; Eng and Bogaert, 2010). Therefore, they can be used by luxury brands for endorsements to create desire for these brands.

This study also reveals an insignificant relationship between purchase intention and actual luxury purchase behavior. Marketers need to introduce entry-level luxury brands and emphasize affordable luxury so that the large middle-class population that aspires to buy luxury brands can turn their aspirations into reality.

The present study demonstrates that luxury buyers in India are young, upwardly mobile, well-educated, and high-earning individuals. Females were found to dominate the Indian luxury scene. Therefore, luxury retailers should develop appropriate strategies and embrace tactical moves to garner the attention of this audience so that they continue to make future purchases and become brand loyal. While designing communication strategies, marketers can target the independent female consumer who makes her own decisions and lives life to the fullest.

### Theoretical Implications

The present research contributes significantly to the existing body of knowledge. Although the theoretical underpinnings of the present work have been widely applied in cross-national contexts to explain varied consumer behavior (Bagozzi et al., 2000; Shen et al., 2003), no study had previously been carried out to explore the impact of beliefs on luxury buying behavior in the Indian context. Based on the findings of the present study, the theory of planned behavior model is modified to better predict luxury buyer behavior in India.

This research also contributes to the extant literature through new findings that provide meaningful insights to academicians and practitioners alike. Findings reveal that normative belief has a direct and significant effect on actual purchase of luxury fashion goods by Indian consumers. It was also found that attitudinal belief is positively correlated with subjective norm and that purchase intention is not a predictor of actual purchase behavior among Indians. While these findings need to be further validated in future studies, the present study provides sound empirical evidence in support of these results.

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

The limitations of the study provide directions for future research. The present work is limited in scope because the sample was derived from a single geographic region: New Delhi, the capital city of India. Therefore, results obtained from this study need to be generalized with caution. Future research may be conducted with a more representative sample, including luxury consumers from other metropolitan cities in India.

At present, the penetration of luxury in India is minuscule. Therefore, a sample size of 257 was considered appropriate. However, in future, with the rapid increase in the number of people consuming luxury brands, research may be undertaken with a larger and more representative sample to better understand consumer behavior for luxury fashion brands in India.

The present research explored new relationships in the context of the theory of planned behavior model, which can be validated by future researchers in this field.
